# Bottleneck Durability Classification

Use this reference to determine whether a stock's bottleneck thesis is **Probably Temporary** or **Potentially Hard to Scale**.

## Contents

1. Core definitions
2. Research model
3. Seven durability tests
4. Company value-capture test
5. Evidence patterns
6. Decision rule
7. Framework sources

## 1. Core definitions

### Probably Temporary

Classify a bottleneck as **Probably Temporary** when price signals, funded capacity, operational normalization, learning, substitution, or new entrants are likely to close the qualified-supply gap within the stated horizon.

Common causes include inventory panic, double ordering, logistics disruption, temporary outages, low initial utilization, short equipment lead times, standardized production, and capacity already under construction.

### Potentially Hard to Scale

Classify a bottleneck as **Potentially Hard to Scale** when required demand is likely to grow faster than qualified supply and practical workarounds because scaling is constrained by elapsed time, physics, geology, specialized equipment, tacit know-how, yield, regulation, permitting, customer qualification, switching costs, or coordination across several scarce layers.

Use “potentially,” not “truly” or “permanently.” Technology and incentives can move or eliminate bottlenecks.

### Distinguish four questions

Answer each separately:

1. Is demand durable?
2. Is the industry constraint difficult to expand?
3. Does the selected company control or solve it?
4. Does the company capture enough value to matter economically?

A “yes” to only the first two does not establish a stock thesis.

## 2. Research model

### Map the complete system

Identify all necessary layers between end demand and delivered output. Estimate the realistic ceiling imposed by each layer. The lowest ceiling is the current binding constraint.

Do not assume the visible or most expensive component is the bottleneck.

### Compare required input with qualified supply

Use this model where data permit:

`Required bottleneck input = End demand × Bottleneck-input intensity per end unit`

`Qualified supply = Nameplate capacity × Yield × Uptime × Customer-qualified share`

`Supply gap = Required bottleneck input − Qualified supply`

Model at least a base case and a supply-response case. Add downside and upside scenarios when uncertainty is material.

Do not substitute announced nameplate capacity for qualified supply.

### Compare the three clocks

1. **Demand clock** — end-demand growth or doubling time.
2. **Qualified-supply clock** — time from funding through construction, tool installation, yield ramp, certification, and customer qualification.
3. **Workaround clock** — time for efficiency, redesign, substitution, recycling, lower specifications, geographic shifts, or vertical integration to reduce dependence on the bottleneck.

Treat the bottleneck as potentially durable only when the demand clock remains faster than both response clocks over the stated horizon.

### State the horizon

Use the industry's real cycle:

- Roughly 12–24 months for many inventory, logistics, and ordinary manufacturing shortages.
- Roughly 3–5 years for mines, fabs, grid equipment, specialized production, regulated capacity, or lengthy qualification.
- Longer only when supported by permitting, geological, clinical, infrastructure, or similar evidence.

These are guides, not universal thresholds.

## 3. Seven durability tests

### Test 1: Multi-constraint test

Analyze each possible constraint independently:

- Raw materials
- Specialized equipment
- Skilled labor
- Yield and scrap
- Power, water, land, or transport
- Construction and commissioning
- Permits and regulation
- Customer certification and qualification
- Working capital and balance-sheet capacity

Determine whether relaxing one layer merely moves the bottleneck elsewhere.

### Test 2: Money-and-time test

Ask: “If the industry received unlimited funding tomorrow, what could it not accelerate?”

Evidence of temporary scarcity:

- Standard equipment from multiple suppliers
- Replicable plant design
- Short hiring and training cycle
- Predictable yield ramp
- Short qualification
- Output roughly proportional to capex

Evidence of structural difficulty:

- Multi-year permits or construction
- Sole-source or capacity-constrained production tools
- Long training and tacit process knowledge
- Yield that depends on accumulated experience
- Long regulatory or customer qualification
- Several suppliers required to expand simultaneously

Capital requirements alone do not prove durability. Measure time to competitive qualified output.

### Test 3: Substitution-and-intensity test

Estimate whether the amount of scarce input required per end unit is falling.

Investigate:

- Software or process efficiency
- Product or system redesign
- Alternative materials or architectures
- Recycling and recovery
- Lower-spec products
- Customer vertical integration
- Geographic substitution

If input intensity can fall faster than end demand grows, classify conservatively.

### Test 4: Entry-barrier test

Evaluate:

- Actual entry and exit history
- Minimum efficient scale relative to total market size
- Upfront losses before reaching scale
- Scarce-resource access
- Proprietary technology and process knowledge
- Customer switching and requalification costs
- Distribution access
- Regulation and industrial policy

Prefer observed entrant outcomes over management descriptions of complexity.

### Test 5: Full-cycle test

Study at least one prior high-price or high-demand cycle when available:

- Capacity announced versus completed
- Time to target yield and qualification
- Lead-time and price normalization
- Entrant market-share gains
- Inventory and utilization behavior
- Incumbent margins and returns after the peak

Repeated overbuilding and margin collapse are strong evidence of cyclicality unless the current structure has verifiably changed.

### Test 6: Bottleneck-migration test

Identify plausible next constraints. Determine whether the company remains relevant when the current bottleneck moves.

Look for transferable:

- Process knowledge
- Installed base and service relationships
- Customer qualification
- Proprietary tools or data
- Adjacent products
- Control points used across multiple architectures

Penalize single-product exposure to a constraint that customers are actively engineering around.

### Test 7: Value-capture test

Verify whether scarcity produces economic benefit for the company:

- Sustained pricing power
- Gross-margin resilience
- Customer deposits, prepayments, or take-or-pay terms
- Backlog conversion into revenue and cash
- Attractive returns on incremental invested capital
- Market-share stability
- Low dependence on one customer or supplier

Backlog alone is insufficient. It may contain cancellable orders, duplicate orders, or low-margin work.

## 4. Company value-capture test

Classify the industry's constraint and the company's position separately before assigning the stock-level indicator.

Use:

- `Company capture: Strong` when the company controls an unavoidable layer and verified economics show bargaining power.
- `Company capture: Mixed` when exposure is real but customers, suppliers, capital intensity, or competition absorb much of the value.
- `Company capture: Weak` when the company depends on the constraint, sells a commodity response, or lacks evidence of pricing and returns.

Require `Strong` or well-supported `Mixed` capture for a `Potentially Hard to Scale` stock-level indicator. A hard industry problem with weak capture must be `Probably Temporary` for the stock thesis.

## 5. Evidence patterns

| Probably Temporary evidence | Potentially Hard to Scale evidence |
|---|---|
| Funded projects close the modeled gap | Gap persists after realistic funded projects |
| Lead time driven by inventory or logistics | Lead time driven by build, yield, permit, or qualification |
| Standardized technology and multiple vendors | Proprietary, yield-sensitive, tacit, or sole-source input |
| Fast second sourcing or redesign | Few acceptable substitutes and long requalification |
| Successful new entrants | Repeated entry or scale-up failures |
| Falling spot prices and lead times | Long contracts, customer funding, or durable pricing |
| Margin spike only at peak utilization | Strong returns survive normalized utilization |
| Market share moves readily | Share remains stable despite strong incentives to enter |

Search specifically for:

- Capacity projects under construction
- Tool delivery schedules
- Yield and utilization
- Customer qualification timing
- Second-source announcements
- Inventory days and cancellations
- Spot versus contract pricing
- Input intensity and efficiency trends
- New technology roadmaps
- Entrant progress and setbacks

## 6. Decision rule

Assign **Potentially Hard to Scale** only when all five conditions below are supported:

1. A non-capital time gate is material.
2. Qualified supply remains slower than required demand after realistic expansion.
3. Workarounds remain slower than demand.
4. Entry barriers have observed or strongly evidenced support.
5. The company controls the constraint and captures value.

Otherwise assign **Probably Temporary**.

Also assign confidence:

- **High** — multiple independent sources agree, current operating evidence supports the conclusion, and counterevidence is weak.
- **Medium** — the core case is supported but one important clock or company-capture mechanism remains uncertain.
- **Low** — data are sparse, management-dependent, contradictory, or highly scenario-sensitive.

When evidence is insufficient, set the indicator to `Probably Temporary` and confidence to `Low`.

For every decision, state:

- The research horizon
- Strongest supporting evidence
- Strongest counterevidence
- Observable conditions that would flip the indicator

## 7. Framework sources

Use these sources as analytical foundations, not as evidence for a current stock unless the source directly addresses it:

- **Epoch AI — “Can AI Scaling Continue Through 2030?”** Model multiple constraints separately, incorporate planned supply expansion and technical progress, and identify the lowest combined ceiling.  
  https://epoch.ai/publications/can-ai-scaling-continue-through-2030

- **Google DeepMind — “An Empirical Analysis of Compute-Optimal Large Language Model Training”** Test whether redesigning the production recipe can substitute between inputs and reduce dependence on an apparent bottleneck.  
  https://deepmind.google/blog/an-empirical-analysis-of-compute-optimal-large-language-model-training/

- **Howard Marks, Oaktree — “More on Repealing the Laws of Economics”** Begin with the presumption that high prices and rewards attract supply, capital, labor, and innovation; require a specific reason that response will remain slow.  
  https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/more-on-repealing-the-laws-of-economics

- **Michael Mauboussin and Counterpoint Global, Morgan Stanley Investment Management — “Measuring the Moat”** Examine actual entry and exit, minimum efficient scale, capital requirements, scarce resources, switching costs, regulation, and sustained returns.  
  https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/article_measuringthemoat.pdf

- **Baillie Gifford — “Under the Radar: Scalability” and “The Next AI Bottleneck”** Monitor how bottlenecks move and distinguish technical importance from company scalability, competitive advantage, value capture, and valuation.  
  https://www.bailliegifford.com/en/ireland/professional-investor/insights/ic-article/2020-q1-under-the-radar-part-4-scalability-10009603/  
  https://www.bailliegifford.com/en/uk/individual-investors/insights/ic-article/2026-q2-ewit-enlightenment-the-next-ai-bottleneck-10063746/

Keep these fund-manager views distinct from independently verified facts. Check live company and industry sources for every report.
